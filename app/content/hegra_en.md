# Hegra

Picture a wide desert plain, sandstone outcrops rising from the sand like islands, and cut into every one of them, a monumental facade with columns, crow-step carvings, and doorways leading nowhere but a small burial chamber.

This is Hegra, also known as Al-Hijr or Mada'in Salih, once the second city of the Nabataean Kingdom after Petra itself. More than a hundred tombs survive here, many with inscriptions naming the family that carved them and the date it was finished - a rare gift to historians trying to piece together who the Nabataeans really were.

The tombs cluster in groups scattered across the plain, each outcrop telling its own story: some facades are simple and unfinished, others elaborately decorated with eagles, sphinxes, and floral crowns marking the wealth and status of the family inside. Unlike Petra, Hegra was a waypoint rather than a capital, a resting point on the incense trade route running north from southern Arabia to the Mediterranean.

For centuries the site sat largely untouched, a place local tradition treated with caution rather than curiosity. In 2008 it became Saudi Arabia's first UNESCO World Heritage Site, and in recent years the wider region around AlUla has opened its doors to visitors for the first time in generations.

**Stand among the tombs of Hegra, and you are standing in a Nabataean city that time - and, for a long while, the world - simply passed by.**
